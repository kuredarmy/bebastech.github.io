Laila had just ticked off her credit card bill when she heard the knock. She set her pen down on the ledger, glanced at the wall clock, then stood up. Past nine. There were usually no visitors at this hour.

Outside stood a man with a helmet in his hand.

"You're the shoe repairman at the market, right?"

"Yes, Bu Laila. Sorry for coming so late at night."

He nodded several times, though Laila had only asked once. His thumb tried to tuck the helmet strap's end into its buckle. It slipped. He tried again.

"Where's your stall now?"

"Closed, Bu. I'm a construction laborer now."

Laila remembered the empty rack beneath the market stairs. Nearly a month ago she had passed by and found the spot swept clean. At the time, all she had wondered was where she would take her shoes if the stitching came undone again.

The man began speaking before she could ask anything else.

"My child's in the hospital, Bu. My wife is still there. I've been…"

He stopped, drew a breath, then repeated that his wife was waiting. The words came fast, occasionally catching. There were traces of cement on his trousers. Both his hands trembled as he shifted the helmet from one hand to the other.

"I'm still short four hundred thousand. If you could help. I don't know where else to look anymore."

Laila held the edge of the door.

In all the time she had known him, she was used to seeing those hands hold a shoe firmly, turn it over, point out the damaged part. Once she had come complaining that her work shoes had split open at the side again. He had taken them, examined the stitching, then told her to sit down first.

"Still wearable, Bu."

Those shoes were now on the rack by the door.

"How long has your child been there?"

"Since yesterday, Bu."

Laila nodded. She listened to what followed, but the figure of four hundred thousand had already pulled part of her mind back to the dining table.

The month was only three days old. Her salary had just come in. In the ledger lying open earlier, the money was already divided: groceries, electricity, commuting, bills. At forty-five, Laila still occasionally covered shortfalls with her credit card. Last month, two grocery runs had gone on it. She had just ticked off the bill.

The four hundred thousand was there. She knew exactly where she kept it.

"I was…"

She stopped. The sentence — that she didn't have the money — had almost slipped out on its own.

The man waited.

"One moment, Bang."

Laila went inside and opened a drawer. Inside was the grocery envelope she had just filled. She took out four hundred-thousand notes and smoothed them across her palm.

If she gave all of it, she could still get to work tomorrow. The electricity was paid. There was still rice in the kitchen. For that night, giving it all felt entirely possible.

Then she counted the days until the next payday. Nearly a month. The groceries weren't done. There were always needs that slipped past her list. They usually surfaced near month's end, when she stood at the register choosing the credit card again.

She glanced at the ledger on the table. One number would have to change, and then another would follow.

From the porch came the sound of the man clearing his throat.

Laila wanted to bring out all four notes. She also wished, for just a moment, that he hadn't come tonight. The thought made her look down at the money in her hands.

It had been a long time since she gave beyond her planned expenses. Bad news usually arrived through chat groups. She would read a few lines, close it, and return to her own affairs. Once she had felt guilty for forgetting so quickly. Lately, she didn't even think about the habit anymore.

Tonight the person was still waiting on her porch.

Laila imagined having to come to someone's house at nine at night, name the amount she needed, then stand silent while the person went inside to decide. Repeating news about her own child to someone she had only ever met for work.

She wasn't sure she could do it. After being turned away a few times, she wasn't sure she would keep choosing the path she had always considered right. From inside her own home, it was so easy to say people should find another way.

She separated two notes from the four in her hand. Two she returned to the envelope. The other two she folded and carried out.

She had prepared an explanation: it was early in the month, her needs were many, she could only give half. At the door, the sentence felt very long.

"Here, Bang."

The man received the money with both hands. Without unfolding it, without looking at how much it was, he slipped it straight into his pocket.

"Thank you, Bu. Thank you."

"That…"

The man was already nodding his farewell. Laila let him put on his helmet. This time she waited until the strap was buckled before wishing his child a swift recovery.

After the motorcycle faded into the distance, Laila stood a while longer at the door. She hadn't managed to explain the amount she had given. She also didn't know where he would go to find the rest.

At the table, she added an expense of two hundred thousand. In the description column, the tip of her pen stopped.

The man knew her name. She had never asked for his. Even now, she still didn't know it.

She wrote: the shoe repairman.

Then she opened the drawer again. The two hundred-thousand notes she had returned earlier were still lying on top of the grocery envelope. She pushed them to the bottom of the envelope, counted the money remaining inside, then matched it against the new figure in the ledger.

The math was right. She checked it once more.
